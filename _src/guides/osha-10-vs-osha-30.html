The short version

I get this question more than any other, usually from someone who's just been told by a foreman or a GC that they "need their OSHA card." The answer depends on one thing: are you responsible for other people's safety on the job?

  • If you're a laborer, apprentice, new hire, warehouse associate, or tradesperson doing the work yourself, OSHA 10 is the standard.
  • If you're a foreman, superintendent, crew lead, safety coordinator, project manager, or business owner, OSHA 30 is the right fit.

What OSHA 10 covers

OSHA 10 is built to give a worker the vocabulary and awareness to spot hazards and speak up. In the Construction version, most of the required time goes to:

  • Introduction to OSHA: your rights, employer responsibilities, how to file a complaint, and how inspections work.
  • The Focus Four hazards: falls, struck-by, caught-in or between, and electrocution, which together cause most construction deaths. (Read more in our Focus Four guide.)
  • PPE and health hazards: hard hats, eye and hearing protection, silica, noise, and chemicals.
  • Electives: topics like scaffolds, ladders, stairways, materials handling, and excavation, chosen by the trainer to fit the group.

The General Industry version follows the same idea with topics like walking-working surfaces, emergency action plans, electrical safety, and hazard communication.

What OSHA 30 adds

OSHA 30 is not just "OSHA 10, three times longer." The extra 20 hours go into topics a supervisor needs to plan and run safe work:

  • Deeper coverage of fall protection, excavations and trenching, scaffolding, and electrical systems.
  • Cranes, rigging, concrete and masonry, and steel erection (Construction), or lockout/tagout, confined spaces, and powered industrial trucks (General Industry).
  • Safety and health programs, hazard prevention planning, and managing subcontractors.
  • Recordkeeping basics and what happens during and after an OSHA inspection.

From the field: The supervisors who get the most out of OSHA 30 are the ones who bring real problems from their own site. Bring your questions about that one ladder setup or trench that always worries you. That's what the class is for.

Who requires which card?

Federal OSHA does not require workers to hold an Outreach card. But plenty of others do:

  • States: Several states, including New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, and Rhode Island, require OSHA 10 for certain construction work, especially public projects. Some require OSHA 30 for supervisors.
  • Cities: New York City, for example, has its own Site Safety Training requirements that build on OSHA courses.
  • Contractors and owners: Many general contractors, school districts, hospitals, and large facilities require OSHA 10 for everyone on site and OSHA 30 for supervisors.
  • Unions and employers: Many build Outreach training into apprenticeships and onboarding.

How to choose for your crew

For employers, the most common setup I see works well:

  1. Put every field worker through OSHA 10 in the version that matches your work.
  2. Put foremen, leads, and anyone who signs off on work through OSHA 30.
  3. Back it up with regular toolbox talks and task-specific training, since OSHA standards require training on specific hazards (like fall protection or forklifts) that an Outreach course alone does not satisfy.
